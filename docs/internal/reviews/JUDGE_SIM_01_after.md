## What all six said, in plain words

These are read across the six write-ups below; each point is made by at least four judges.

1. **No human evidence yet.** All six say it first: nobody has taken the test, the live creek has no visits, so the central claim, that a per-feature score tells a city how far to trust an answer, is untested. Five of six name the same change: report the human row after the lock on Sep 28, even a small, descriptive one, and put it on the first screen.
2. **The README's opening is dense.** Four judges could not tell in 30 seconds what a judge can click or what the AI does for a volunteer today; the first lines carry FHIR words (a test sitting, a dated qualification, Provenance) before the problem is plain, and the README is long for a judge with minutes.
3. **The AI part never reaches a person today.** The checker is off on the live site, none of the three walks carries a checker question, and every model failed plants because the photos are close-ups and the instruction says can't tell when no stream is in view. The gate is well tested, but what it guards is mostly in the future.
4. **The test is small and local to California.** Four photos per feature, gold labels from one person working with a Claude chat, and California plants, for a project whose cities are in Europe.

What they liked, in the same way: the opening pair of photos, which makes the point in two seconds; the honesty (it says nobody has taken the test, and the AI must earn the right to ask, feature by feature, on the people's test); and the engineering a judge can check: the live API answers, 0 validator errors against the pinned guide, about 2000 tests, results graded again from the raw replies, and the contribution back to the guide.

What moved since the baseline: every judge door the baseline found broken now works (the city view, the lab viewer, the walks, `/verify`), the results exist and are graded again from their raw replies, and the screenshots show the real product. Technical went from 4.67 to 8.00, and the weighted mean from 5.73 to 7.33. Impact moved least, 6.00 to 7.00, and the judges say why: no people yet.

## Freshwater ecologist who leads the project

- **Impact, 7.** It takes on the Track 3 problem head on. It teaches the four things volunteers really do walk past (concrete banks, dug-out channels, plants that do not belong, pipes) and attaches a per-feature score to each observation inside OneAquaHealth's own FHIR profiles, which is a useful quality mark for citizen data that sits next to lab data. What holds it back: no person has taken the test yet, so nothing shows the lesson improves detection or that the score predicts field accuracy, and the team's own simulation says four photos per feature is too coarse to weight votes.
- **Innovation, 8.** The best idea here is making the AI pass the same 16-photo test as the volunteers before it may ask about a feature, with the person always answering first. That is an original, easy-to-explain form of human in the loop. Observer accreditation itself is borrowed from the UK River Habitat Survey, and they say so; carrying it through Provenance to every Observation is new for volunteer data.
- **Technical, 8.** The engineering is very strong. It has a live Cloudflare site with working API routes (/api/city and /api/two returned real JSON), 0 validator errors against the OAH guide, about 2000 tests, mutation testing, Python and TypeScript kept equal by golden vectors, and results that rebuild from committed raw replies. The AI side is thin in practice, though. The checker is off on the live site and 0 of 3 walks ever shows a checker question. The benchmark is 16 photos, and every model scored 0 of 12 on plants because the plant photos are close-ups where "no stream in view" pushes the model to can't tell.
- **Usability, 7.** The volunteer flow looks clean and easy on a phone: a strong "Which creek is healthier?" opener, lesson cards with numbered marks, big Yes/No/Can't tell buttons and a clear score per feature. Today, though, the analyst side shows nothing live (the city page says nobody has checked the creek, and /two only has their example because the OAH sandbox is down). Judge mode is closed, a judge who takes the test becomes a study session, and the 638-line README is very hard to take in with only minutes.
- **Feasibility, 7.** It is cheap to run (free Cloudflare tier) and uses OAH's own profiles, with a follower-city script and a pull request and issues sent to the hl7-eu/oah guide, so integration is plausible. Weak points for real use: the volunteer is modelled as a Practitioner as a workaround, one person set the labels, and the test photos and plant list are Californian, not from the creeks where OAH's European cities would use it. A quiz before every volunteer's first report also adds friction nobody has measured.

Three things they would find thin:

1. No human evidence yet: zero sessions, so the central claim, that a two-minute score tells an analyst how far to trust an answer, is untested. There is also no field check that a high scorer is actually more accurate at a real creek.
2. The test itself is small and narrow: 4 photos per feature, gold labels from one person working with a Claude chat, and plant items that are California iNaturalist close-ups (e.g. Himalayan vs native blackberry). For a European project, plants that do not belong is a regional botany question, and a single photo can decide a pass.
3. The AI part never reaches a user: the checker is off on the live site, no walk carries a checker question, and all four models failed plants because of how the items and instruction interact. The gate is well tested, but what it guards is mostly hypothetical today.

One change that would raise a score: Usability from 7 to 8: open the README with five plain sentences (the problem, what was built, the one AI result, the link to the test, what is not done yet) and move the gate, FHIR and mutation tables below that, so a judge sees the whole story in one screen.

What they did not understand in the first 30 seconds: What it means for a model to have "passed" a feature, and how much evidence a pass needs (it turns out to be all four photos right in two of three runs). What "stores their per-feature score in the record of their test sitting, with a dated qualification, both linked by Provenance" means for someone who does not know FHIR. Which of the Numbers at a glance rows (gate drops, agreement on unlabelled frames, cost per 100 frames, mutation kills) tells me whether the thing works. Whether Can't tell counts against a volunteer (it does, as wrong).

What they liked: The opening pair of photos makes the point in two seconds: the tidy park is hiding a concrete channel, and the messy bend is the healthier creek. The project is also unusually honest: it says outright that nobody has taken the test yet, and it makes the AI earn the right to ask, feature by feature, on the same test as the people.

## HL7 and FHIR standards fellow

- **Impact, 7.** It goes straight at the Track 3 problem: it measures how well each volunteer sees each kind of creek damage and stores that score in OneAquaHealth's own FHIR profiles next to their observations. - **Innovation, 8.** The AI sits the same 16-photo test as the volunteers and earns the right to ask one question, feature by feature, only after the person has answered. That is an original human-in-the-loop design. Bringing surveyor accreditation (River Habitat Survey) to volunteers and carrying it through Practitioner.qualification plus Provenance is a fresh use of FHIR in citizen science.
- **Technical, 8.** The live Worker API answers (/api/city, /api/two, /api/fhir/validation). 14 records validate against the pinned OAH guide with 0 errors and terminology checking on.

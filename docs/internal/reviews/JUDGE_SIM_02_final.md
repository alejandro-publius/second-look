## What all six said, in plain words

These are read across the six write-ups below; each point is made by at least four judges.

- The entry goes straight at the track's problem. Volunteers differ in how well they spot creek damage, so the team scores each volunteer on each feature and keeps that score with every record in OneAquaHealth's own FHIR profiles.
- Nobody has used it yet. There are zero test sessions from real people and zero real creek visits, and the Strawberry Creek city view shows 0 visits. So the main claim, that the score tells someone how far to trust an observation, is untested.
- The team's own simulation works against that claim. It found that four photos per feature is too coarse to weight answers by, and a plain majority did as well or better.
- The most original idea is making the AI sit the same photo test as the volunteers. It may ask a follow-up only on features it passed, and only after the person has answered, and the record is built from human answers alone.
- What is new is the combination, not the parts. Observer accreditation, photo quizzes, vision models and FHIR all exist already.
- The AI is switched off on the live site, so a volunteer never sees it do anything today, and no model passed the invasive plants feature. In their first minute, every judge wondered what the AI actually does in an AI track entry.
- The test is small and rests on one person. It has 16 photos, four per feature, and a single labeller set the right answers with no second opinion.
- The engineering is far above hackathon level. Records pass the HL7 validator with 0 errors, shared test cases keep a Python version and a TypeScript version in step, and the tests include mutation testing.
- They gave back to the partner. They sent a pull request and issues to the OneAquaHealth FHIR guide, and they quoted the Policy Brief word for word for the city actions.
- The honesty is unusual and welcome. The README lists its own known weaknesses and keeps what is real apart from what is synthetic.
- The "Which creek is healthier?" opening is a strong hook, and the lesson cards teach well. They show in seconds that a tidy creek can be the damaged one.
- The phone screens are clean and easy to use, with big buttons and one question per screen.
- For a judge it is hard going. The README is very long and full of the team's own terms, judge mode is shut until Sep 28, and the GitHub links return 404 until Sep 30.
- The live pages that should show the whole thing working are empty. The city view has no visits, and the comparison page shows only the team's own example because the partner sandbox is down.
- To see what a city is told after a walk, a judge has to answer as if the creek were damaged. The walk record screenshot shows a "no network" message, which looks like an error in a showcase image.
- Running it would cost almost nothing. It runs on the free Cloudflare tier, has a one-command setup for a new city, and writes records in the partner's own profiles.
- It is not ready for a real city yet. The daily jobs run on one team member's laptop, the question wording is a draft that has not been checked against the official app, it is English only, and no city or volunteer group has taken it up.
- The official app has no question for dug-out channels. So one of the four features the test scores leads to nothing in the creek check.
- Once judge mode opens, anyone can rebuild the answer key. That means a stored score can be gamed.
- Every judge made the same request: put real people through the test before judging, even a small group, and show a real record with the observer's score beside it. Most said this would raise Impact from 7 to 8.

## Freshwater ecologist who leads the project

- **Impact, 7.** It goes straight at a problem we actually have. Volunteers report smell, foam and colour well and walk past concrete banks, dredged channels, invasive plants and outfalls, so a tidy creek gets a good rating it does not deserve. They built on the four features I named in the workshop, on our FHIR guide, on the question order of our citizen app and on the restoration measures from our Policy Brief page 9. The dry-weather pipe question leading to a lab ServiceRequest is a real One Health link from a citizen to the water, to people and to dogs. What holds it at 7 is that nothing has been measured on a person yet: zero sessions, zero real visits, the Strawberry Creek city view is empty, and their own simulation says weighting by a 4-photo score does worse than a plain majority. So the central claim, that the score tells an analyst how far to trust an observation, is still a hypothesis.
- **Innovation, 8.** Making the AI sit the same photo test as the volunteers, and only letting it ask a follow-up on a feature it passed, is an original and sensible way to govern a vision model in citizen science. I have not seen it done before. Storing a per-feature observer qualification, linked through Provenance to every Observation, is also a fresh idea for hydromorphology, though the general idea of weighting observers by skill already exists elsewhere (eBird observer models, RHS surveyor accreditation, which they cite). Using Open-Meteo dry days to decide when a flowing pipe is worth a question is a clever, cheap version of illicit discharge screening.
- **Technical, 8.** The engineering is far above what I expect from a hackathon: records validated against our guide with the HL7 validator at 0 errors, a Python reference and a TypeScript Worker held equal by golden vectors, mutation testing, a pre-registered plan stamped in Bitcoin, and a pull request plus three issues sent back to hl7-eu/oah (the morophology typo and the SpecimenOah collector gap are real finds). It loses points on the part the track is named for: the checker is off on the live site, no model passed invasive plants, the kept flags on dug-out channels ask nothing because our app has no such question, and the one kept flag they show is a model calling stones in a creek bed a built bank. Gold labels come from one person with no blind second labeller.
- **Usability, 7.** The phone screens are clean and plain. Big buttons, one question per screen, an Enlarge button, and lesson cards that pair 'what people assume' with 'what is there' with numbered marks, which is good teaching. The landing hook 'Which creek is healthier?' works. For a judge it is harder: the README runs past 700 lines with its own glossary of terms (arm, lock, gate, pass table, the Mac), judge mode is shut until Sep 28, the GitHub links on /judges are private until Sep 30, /city and /spot are empty live, and to see what a city is told from a walk I am asked to answer as if the creek were damaged. The walk record screenshot in the gallery shows a 'no network' message, which is an odd choice for the showcase.
- **Feasibility, 6.** Hosting is free, it works offline after one visit, it speaks our FHIR profiles, and there is a one-command recipe to scaffold a new city. Against that: Berkeley is not one of our pilot sites and has not adopted it, the daily jobs and backups run on one team member's laptop, it is English only while our pilots are across Europe, the question wording is still marked draft against our app, our sandbox has been unreachable so the mirror is untested lately, and 16 fixed photos mean the answer key can be rebuilt after the lock, so the stored qualification could be gamed. A real programme would need a much larger, local, rotating item bank and a second labeller before I would put its scores on records.

Three things they would find thin:

1. No human evidence yet. Zero test sessions, zero real creek visits, and their own synthetic simulation says per-feature weights from 4 photos lose to a plain majority, so the claim that the score tells an analyst how much weight to give an observation is untested and partly undercut.
2. The item bank is too small and not local. Four photos per feature (two present, two absent), labelled by one person, from several countries and seasons; invasive plant status is regional, so a 'plant that does not belong' in a UK photo says little about a Bay Area or Mediterranean volunteer. A '4 of 4 on built banks' qualification on a record looks more precise than it is.
3. The AI does nothing a volunteer sees today. The checker is off live, no model passed invasive plants, 32 of 35 kept footage flags are on dug-out channels that the check never asks about, and the showcase kept flag is a false positive on stones in a creek bed. For an AI-Supported Assessment track the AI is a well-built cage around a model that is not yet earning its place.

One change that would raise a score: Run the test with one real volunteer group before judging closes (a Strawberry Creek stewardship group or citizen scientists at one of our pilot sites, even 30 to 40 people across both arms), have a second ecologist label the 16 photos blind, and report per-feature scores lesson-first versus photos-first. That would raise Impact from 7 to 8, because the core claim would rest on people rather than on a plan and a simulation.

What they did not understand in the first 30 seconds: The README opens with a line of track text before the title and then a pitch, then a glossary of nine terms, which told me this would be dense. I did not understand at first what an analyst is supposed to do with '3 of 4 on built banks' beside an answer: trust it, discard it, weight it? The later admission that weighting did worse than majority left that open. I also did not see at first why an AI track entry has its AI switched off on the live site, whether taking the test as a judge pollutes their study (it does count as a session), and why the photos might come before the lesson for me (that is the randomized arm, explained only further down).

What they liked: The dry-weather pipe question: only asking whether a pipe is running after N dry days from Open-Meteo, and turning two passed observers seeing it into a lab ServiceRequest, is sound field logic for finding illicit discharges and a real bridge from citizen to lab. The lesson cards teach the right thing, that a slumped earth bank on a bend can be natural and a mown park can hide a concrete channel, though I would add that heavy erosion can also be a sign of urban flashiness. They used our Policy Brief measures word for word with page citations, modelled Strawberry Creek as nested reaches down to the West Berkeley culvert, and contributed back to our implementation guide with a pull request and issues. The honesty is unusual and welcome: a real versus synthetic table, a long known weaknesses list, and showing a kept flag that is probably wrong instead of hiding it.

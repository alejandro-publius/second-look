## Built banks, and the laid stone question

**Protocols, page 13**, "Substrate in the banks", quoted:

> EA: Earth/soil; ST: Layed Stones (natural stones not compacted by concrete); GA: Gabion
> (non-natural stones or from other areas, not compacted by concrete, inside a metal mesh);
> CC: concrete or similar artificial impervious materials; Other: specify

This settles Update 09 section 4.3 from the project's own protocol, not from our reading of the
River Habitat Survey. Laid stone (ST) is its own category, separate from concrete (CC) and
separate from earth (EA). So laid stone is not earth, which is why we do not call it natural, and
it is not concrete, which is why no laid stone photo goes in the test set. The protocol also
records each margin separately and uses Present, Extensive (more than 33 percent) or Absent.

Same page, the pipe definition:

> Outflows (number of pipes or similar discharging pluvial waters to the stream)

and, separately, Intakes for pipes abstracting water. Our feature is the outflow.

## Plants that do not belong

**Protocols, page 14** records, for each channel vegetation type, whether there are native or
non-native species, with abundance A, P or E and a count of non-native species. Riparian
vegetation is recorded by cover category in 20 percent bands, which is the same banding their
FHIR profile uses.

**Factsheets, page 13**, indicator XI, Invasive Alien Plants of the riparian corridor, quoted in
part:

> Invasive Alien Plants (IAP) are plant species introduced, intentionally or accidentally,
> outside their natural geographic range that spread rapidly and cause ecological, economic,
> and/or social harm.

and, on why a volunteer seeing one matters:

> These species tend to thrive in disturbed, nutrient-enriched, or poorly managed environments
> and their presence can reflect habitat disturbance (e.g., soil movement, bank erosion,
> vegetation clearing), hydrological alteration (e.g., modified flow regimes, channelization),
> nutrient enrichment or pollution.

and on the health link, which is the one our approved sentence rests on:

> In terms of health impacts, AIPs, particularly angiosperms, are frequently associated with the
> production of allergenic pollen and toxic compounds, which can cause wounds, skin irritation,
> allergic reactions and poisoning. Additionally, IAPs may act as mediators of disease vectors
> (e.g., dipteran vectors, ticks) and allow the occurrence and proliferation of vector-borne
> diseases.

Note what this does and does not say. It is a statement about invasive alien plants in general.
It is not a statement about any particular creek. Our rule that nothing states a risk for a
specific site still holds, and a sentence that cites this must be about the plant, not the place.

## Pipes, sewage and what can be in the water

**Factsheets, page 10**, indicator VIII, Pathogens, quoted in part:

> These organisms are naturally present in the environment at low levels, but their abundance can
> increase due to pollution, wastewater discharge, or environmental disturbances.

and:

> High levels of pathogens in water can indicate contamination from sewage, agricultural runoff,
> or wildlife, posing risks for drinking water, recreation, and aquaculture.

Again: this is about what raised pathogen levels mean, measured in a laboratory. A volunteer
seeing a pipe has not measured anything. Any sentence we show has to stay on the side of "this is
worth a second look", never "this water is unsafe".

## What the collection is for, in their words

**Factsheets, page 2**:

> The OneAquaHealth project aims to identify and refine indicators at both ecosystem and
> biological levels that can reflect the potential risks to human and animal health originating
> from degraded freshwater ecosystems in urban areas.

That sentence is the whole reason a citizen observation is worth a trust score.

## Channel form

The protocols record substrate types present in the channel with an Artificial (AR) category
alongside bedrock, boulders, stones, gravel, sand, mud and organic matter (page 13), and cover
hydromorphology on pages 2 to 4 and 12. There is no single "dug out channel" item, which is why
our fourth question uses our own wording rather than theirs.
